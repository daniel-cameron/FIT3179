Public Transport in Australia — who really gets a seat?

Victoria’s Public Transport in Context

Public transport in Victoria has undergone dramatic changes over recent years. From population growth to pandemic disruptions, this analysis visualises how Victorians travel, identifies where congestion occurs, and highlights what times of day and year are most affected.

By exploring these patterns, the visualisations guide the reader through both everyday commuting trends and seasonal shifts in ridership, providing a clear understanding of Victoria’s evolving public transport landscape.

The Current Situation

Across Victoria, millions depend on trains, trams, and buses daily. The isotype chart illustrates the distribution of travellers per mode, highlighting the dominance of metropolitan rail networks.

According to the chart, Victorians take up to 16 million train trips per month. While this may seem substantial, consider that Victoria’s population was 6.959 million in the recent census. This reveals that the average Victorian takes just over 2 train rides per month.

The chart also highlights the regional–metropolitan discrepancy: limited public transport accessibility in rural Victoria makes cars the preferred mode of transport, while urban areas rely heavily on trains and trams.

Where Congestion Happens

The proportional bubble map illustrates which stations experience the heaviest congestion. Larger bubbles indicate higher annual passenger entries, while colour differentiates between weekday and weekend dominance.

Key trends emerge from the bubble chart: station entries are heavily skewed towards those closer to the Melbourne CBD, highlighting that public transport is less frequently used in the outer suburbs. Large stations such as Caulfield and Richmond see significantly higher commuter numbers on weekends, likely due to their proximity to the Caulfield Racecourse and the MCG respectively.

With train usage higher across the eastern suburbs than the west, it remains to be seen whether the proposed Suburban Rail Loop will help reduce congestion and shorten travel times for commuters across Melbourne.

Daily View: Where and When Congestion Happens

As shown in the heatmap, the busiest period for train passengers is the evening peak (4–7pm), when commuters travel home from work. In contrast, morning hours (before 9am) are noticeably quieter across most stations, reflecting the growing trend of staggered work hours and hybrid commuting patterns since the pandemic.

Congestion is most pronounced in the CBD, while suburban stations such as Footscray and Caulfield experience a more even spread of commuters throughout the day.

Where Do People Go During Holidays?

School holidays bring noticeable shifts in station traffic. Stations linked to schools, like Huntingdale and Riversdale, experience large drops in daily passengers—Huntingdale alone loses over 2,000 riders per day. Even Flemington Racecourse sees an 80% drop-off while school kids are on break.

Conversely, stations near leisure destinations benefit from the holidays. Southland and the Showgrounds see noticeable increases in traffic as families head out for shopping and events.

Overall, the barbell chart highlights these contrasting impacts: commuter-heavy, school-adjacent stations fall, while fun and recreational destinations rise.

Overall Challenges and Trends

To view how different transport modes have changed over time over the last 7 years, see the line graph. Click specific transport modes on the legend to view them in isolation.

The COVID-19 pandemic sharply reduced passenger numbers across all transport modes. Although recovery is underway, the data highlights sustained changes in commuting patterns, particularly in off-peak and regional services. Even so, passenger numbers from 2018 remain higher than those in 2025, indicating long-term shifts in travel behaviour.

Increasing public transport usage is critical for Australia to reduce traffic congestion, greenhouse gas emissions, and urban sprawl. Encouraging commuters to rely on trains, trams, and buses instead of cars will improve city liveability and sustainable mobility for future generations.
